Nguồn: Light Reading, đăng 24/8/2026

Peeking out from behind the vast bulk of Huawei, like a toddler obscured by a bigger sibling, ZTE is easy to miss as the other member of the Chinese group that the European Union disparagingly dubs the "high-risk vendors." In terms of sales, it is less than one-sixth Huawei's size, based on last year's revenues. State-controlled, it can sometimes appear to exist as a check on Huawei, a government effort to ensure the bigger Chinese player does not become all-powerful in a domestic market that has been squeezing out foreign rivals. But it remains the world's fourth-largest vendor of the radio access network products that support today's 5G services.

That is remarkable given that ZTE's very survival was in doubt eight years ago. According to the first Trump administration, it had allegedly breached US sanctions by shipping goods to perennial foe Iran. Hit with successive billion-dollar fines, ZTE was also named on a US trade blacklist, cutting it off from vital components made by American suppliers. Forced to cease operations, it looked to be on the verge of collapse before Trump rode to the rescue, brandishing a settlement deal with Xi Jinping, his Chinese counterpart, to save jobs.

ZTE suffered a crushing net loss of about half a billion US dollars in 2018 on sales of roughly 85.5 billion Chinese yuan (US$12.7 billion). But its controversial removal from that trade blacklist, which seems to have been largely forgotten, allowed it to recover and thrive in subsequent years. By 2023, ZTE's revenues had climbed to more than RMB124 billion ($18.5 billion), while its net profit reached almost RMB7.4 billion ($1.1 billion). Since then, its fortunes in some areas have darkened.

While sales had increased to a high point of almost RMB134 billion ($19.9 billion) last year, net profit had tumbled to less than RMB3.4 billion ($510 million). Results published late last week show the delta between sales and profits has continued to widen. Revenues for the first half of this year grew 9%, to RMB78 billion ($11.6 billion), but net profit dropped 46%, to around RMB2.2 billion ($330 million).

Geopolitical headwinds are obviously not helping ZTE to advance into potentially lucrative western markets. And if governments are worried about Huawei, they are even more concerned about a company with overt links to the Chinese state. In 2017, ZTE generated a quarter of its revenues – about RMB27.3 billion ($4.1 billion) – from "Europe, Americas and Oceania," a line in its reports encompassing multiple regions. By last year, that contribution was down to less than 15%, or RMB19.4 billion ($2.9 billion).

At one time wholly reliant on ZTE, Italy's Wind Tre turned to Ericsson in 2018 as a second supplier out of fear that US sanctions might finish off its traditional vendor. ZTE does not appear to have regained what it lost. Other telcos across the region may have had similar stories to recount.

Problematically, too, ZTE's main "carrier" business, the part responsible for network products, appears to have stalled and even gone into reverse. As recently as 2023, it still accounted for more than two-thirds of ZTE's revenues, generating nearly RMB83 billion ($12.4 billion). The amount had plummeted by some RMB20 billion ($3 billion) just two years later, lowering its contribution to less than 47% of the 2025 total. For the first half of 2026, carrier revenues fell 9.4% year-over-year, to RMB31.8 billion ($4.7 billion).

To blame is an apparent reduction in spending by China's huge, state-backed telcos. A loss of market share in China by Ericsson and Nokia has not translated into significant gains for ZTE because, as it says in its report, "domestic carriers' investment in communication infrastructure continued to decline." Unfortunately, the resulting drop in revenues affects ZTE's most profitable unit. The carrier group booked a gross margin of more than 38% for the first six months. ZTE's gadget-making consumer group managed only 18% and its unit that serves government and business customers reported a margin of less than 16%.

Of those three units, the government and business group has suddenly emerged as the engine of ZTE's sales growth. Between 2020 and 2023, its annual sales grew at a relatively modest pace, rising from RMB11.3 billion ($1.7 billion) to RMB13.6 billion ($2 billion) over this period. But they jumped to about RMB18.6 billion ($2.8 billion) in 2024 before doubling to RMB37.2 billion ($5.5 billion) last year. For the first half of 2026, growth continued at a year-over-year rate of 43.5%, to RMB27.6 billion ($4.1 billion).

Serving government and corporate clients, however, seems to be a costly and potentially risky endeavor. ZTE's profits suffered mainly due to a sharp rise in the cost of raw materials and other goods needed for high-end servers and AI infrastructure. Unrestrainable demand for that AI infrastructure has notably driven up the price of memory chips used in various IT and networking products to record highs. Prices may well continue to rise, putting even more pressure on ZTE's profits.

But ZTE's ongoing use of American chips denied to other Chinese players looks risky. Last month, ZTE reportedly secured US government approval to buy Nvidia's H200 chips. While these are not state of the art in AI, they may be sufficient for many business needs. The danger is that a capricious US government decides to be even more restrictive in future.

Unlike Huawei, ZTE does not have its own lineup of graphics processing units to offer as substitutes for American products. What's more, its carrier business still seems to be making use of components supplied by companies such as AMD, Intel and Nvidia. ZTE has no qualms about mentioning its involvement in the "x86 general-purpose computing ecosystem and the CUDA-like intelligent computing ecosystem" in commentary about its carrier unit. The x86 reference is to the architecture adopted by Intel and AMD, while CUDA is the software platform of Nvidia.

Whatever one makes of Trump's boast eight years ago that his intervention would save jobs, ordinary employees have been shown the exit in huge numbers since then. ZTE still had nearly 75,000 employees on its books at the end of 2022, according to the annual report for that year. By June 2026, headcount had fallen to less than 63,000, with 2,500 job cuts made in the previous six months alone.

Huawei bucks the trend observable everywhere else, having gained 6,000 employees between 2022 and 2025 to bring its total to about 213,000. For all ZTE's recent sales success in the market for server and AI products, on headcount development it looks strikingly similar to some of its western rivals.
