SK hynix Maps CPO Roadmap as AI Scaling Shifts from Chips to Systems

SK hynix is extending its AI infrastructure strategy beyond High Bandwidth Memory into optical system architecture, co-authoring a technology roadmap that places co-packaged optics at the center of future large-scale AI systems. The peer-reviewed review article, “Co-packaged optics for high-performance computing and artificial intelligence,” was published in Nature Electronics in 2026. It examines how optical compute interconnects can address the growing mismatch between accelerator performance and the bandwidth, power and reach of electrical interconnects. The work brings together SK hynix AI Infra Team lead Seunghoon Hong with researchers from the University of Virginia, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Nanyang Technological University, MIT and Yonsei University.

The central problem is increasingly system-level rather than confined to the accelerator package. HBM has substantially increased bandwidth between processors and local memory, but AI training and inference now distribute computation across thousands of accelerators, creating another data-movement constraint between chips, nodes, racks and pods. SK hynix says compute throughput has historically increased about 3× every two years while interconnect bandwidth has increased only about 1.4×. Copper remains economical for short links, but higher signaling rates and longer reaches increase insertion loss, equalization requirements and power. CPO moves electro-optical conversion close to the processor, minimizing the high-speed electrical path before moving data onto optical links. The researchers identify system targets exceeding 100 Tb/s of aggregate bandwidth per node, energy below 1 pJ/bit and chip-to-chip latency below 10 nanoseconds.

The roadmap goes further than replacing electrical links between processors. Its longer-term architecture extends optics toward the memory interface itself. A proposed photonic-interposer architecture directly connects pools of XPUs and memory resources using optical links, potentially allowing multiple accelerators to access larger shared memory pools without requiring all memory capacity to remain physically adjacent to each processor. This makes CPO part of a broader co-design problem involving memory devices and controllers, photonics, packaging, protocols, thermal engineering and system architecture.

• Research spans optical chip-to-chip interconnects and CPO for HPC and AI systems.

• Roadmap progresses through 2D CPO, 2.5D interposer integration and 3D heterogeneous stacking.

• Target node bandwidth: greater than 100 Tb/s.

• Target interconnect efficiency: below 1 pJ/bit.

• Target chip-to-chip latency: below 10 ns.

• Longer-term architecture extends optical connectivity toward memory.

• Key commercialization issues include thermal management, manufacturing, photonic-device integration, system reliability and standardization.

“Even if computing chips become more powerful, overall system performance cannot improve unless data movement between chips keeps pace,” said Kyusang Lee, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the University of Virginia. “Replacing copper interconnects, which face inherent physical limitations, with optical links is the most promising path toward future scalability.”

From HBM to Optical Memory Architecture

The significance of the work is that it treats the memory wall and the interconnect wall as related system problems. HBM addresses bandwidth immediately adjacent to an accelerator by vertically stacking DRAM and connecting it through very wide interfaces. The CPO roadmap addresses what happens after that local memory interface. Scaling accelerator performance and HBM bandwidth increases the volume of data that must eventually cross package, board, rack and cluster boundaries. At sufficiently high signaling rates, the energy required to move bits becomes an increasingly important part of system power. Moving the electrical-to-optical boundary closer to compute and eventually memory therefore becomes an architectural mechanism for scaling bandwidth without proportionally increasing electrical reach.

The proposed optics-centric architecture takes that reasoning another step by relaxing the assumption that memory must remain tightly constrained by the physical perimeter of the compute package. A photonic interposer could provide optical connectivity between XPU and memory pools, opening a path toward larger and more flexible memory domains. Such an architecture would require considerably more than photonic devices: memory controllers, coherence mechanisms, packaging, switching, software and reliability mechanisms would have to evolve together.

Analysis

SK hynix’s participation in this roadmap is notable because it connects two infrastructure constraints that have often been treated separately: memory bandwidth and network/interconnect bandwidth. HBM can increase bandwidth within the accelerator package, but the benefit becomes progressively harder to exploit if communication among accelerators, memory domains and racks cannot scale at a comparable rate. CPO therefore becomes relevant to a memory manufacturer because the performance of future memory increasingly depends on the architecture used to reach it.

The optics-centric memory concept also places memory disaggregation, Scale Up connectivity and advanced packaging on a converging technical path. The industry is already exploring memory expansion, optical I/O, photonic fabrics and other mechanisms for separating memory capacity from individual processors. SK hynix’s roadmap adds a memory-vendor perspective: optical links could ultimately become part of the memory hierarchy itself rather than simply the network connecting servers.

The roadmap should not be interpreted as a product announcement or deployment schedule. The authors identify thermal management, manufacturability and standardization as unresolved requirements. The architectural direction, however, illustrates a broader change underway in AI infrastructure. Memory, compute, packaging and networking can no longer be optimized independently as accelerator clusters grow.
