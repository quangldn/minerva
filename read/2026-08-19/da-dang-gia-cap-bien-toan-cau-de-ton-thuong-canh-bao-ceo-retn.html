RETN CEO Tony O'Sullivan warned that a lack of meaningful diversity in subsea networks leaves global connectivity vulnerable during an interview with Light Reading.

The industry may be creating a false sense of resilience by deploying more subsea cables along the same geographical routes rather than building genuinely diverse international routes, according to Tony O'Sullivan, CEO of global network services provider RETN.

"If we use the example of the Persian Gulf, we see that adding more cables along the same corridors doesn't guarantee resilience at all, that's 'fake diversity'," O'Sullivan told Light Reading. "Even when operators deploy new subsea or terrestrial routes, they still rarely chart entirely new territory because utilising traditional routes is cheaper in the short term." This results in additional capacity without any improvement in resilience.

Headquartered in the UK, RETN is an independent international network operator that provides high-capacity connectivity across Europe, Asia and North America. Exa Infrastructure, Tata Communications and NTT are some of the companies it competes with.

O'Sullivan's remarks come at a time when network operators and hyperscalers are investing in boosting international connectivity to support growing AI and cloud computing traffic.

Even so, several subsea cable failures over the last few years have exposed the gaps in global Internet backbone network infrastructure. There have been several disruptions to critical international cable systems in the last two years.

For instance, four major subsea cables, SEA-ME-WE 4, FALCON GCX, Europe-India Gateway and IMEWE, were damaged last year, forcing operators to reroute traffic between Europe, the Middle East and Asia. While Internet services remained largely operational thanks to redundancy, the incident highlighted how heavily global connectivity depends on a few maritime routes. In addition, there have been instances of subsea cable damage in the Baltic Sea.

Instead of simply deploying additional capacity along existing routes, O'Sullivan argues the industry needs to build genuinely independent terrestrial and submarine paths that avoid common points of failure.

"What the industry needs is a mix of submarine and terrestrial routes, diversified across geopolitical regions. We need to build out a completely independent, well-thought-out infrastructure, which is expensive. At the same time, it would significantly reduce the risk of common-mode failures," O'Sullivan said.

According to the International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC), a majority of submarine cable faults result from accidental human activity rather than sabotage, which further pushes the case for more diverse routes for subsea cables.

AI raises the stakes

The resilience debate has become urgent as AI redefines global traffic patterns. Unlike traditional traffic patterns, AI is generating two distinct types of traffic: massive bandwidth-intensive flows between data centers used for model training, and latency-sensitive inference traffic generated by real-time AI applications.

According to O'Sullivan, AI-driven traffic has nearly tripled over the past year, while AI agents and automated browsers are generating massive volumes of Machine-to-Machine traffic. At the same time, AI clusters are increasingly being built where electricity is cheapest rather than where users are located, requiring network operators to move much larger volumes of data over longer international distances, which also demands route diversity.

"Customers seek a combination of scale, low latency and resilience," he said, adding that physical and geographic route diversity has become a strategic requirement as AI infrastructure evolves into critical infrastructure.

O'Sullivan believes that AI will be the main driver of the industry over the next five years. "As yet, no one knows exactly how AI will shape networks… All we know is that there will be a huge increase in bandwidth demand for datacenter-to-datacenter connectivity for compute and for edge-to-datacenter connectivity for inference," he stated.

However, he claims the biggest constraint may not be optical technology itself but the industry's ability to source enough hardware to keep pace with AI demand. "Optical transport itself could become a limiting factor, mainly due to supply chain issues on the hardware side, where manufacturers are struggling to keep up with unprecedented AI-driven demand," he said.

"There will be a fundamental shift in what AI computes, from fixed, historical datasets to processing human activity in real time. The original promise of smart cities will begin to be fulfilled, and IoT will finally begin to utilise 5G networks as intended, enabling many more devices to connect simultaneously. This means there will likely be much larger-than-expected traffic growth on the access layer and, therefore, much greater importance on both access and backbone internet connectivity," O'Sullivan added.

To address the surge in AI-driven traffic, RETN is prioritizing the rollout of 400G coherent optics to "enable its backbone to handle AI-era surges while reducing the environmental cost per bit."
